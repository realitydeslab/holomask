\section{Method}

We adopted a Research through Design (RtD) approach~\cite{zimmerman2007research} combined with a two-phase empirical study to investigate temporal wearing as a design dimension for mixed reality. Phase~1 is a controlled lab study that isolates the effects of form factor and transition cost under controlled conditions, including a session-wear baseline for comparison. Phase~2 is an in-situ deployment that examines how temporal wearing behaviors emerge naturalistically in a public setting. This two-phase design addresses a key limitation identified in prior work on novel wearable form factors~\cite{khurana2019detachable, gemperle1998wearability}: controlled studies establish internal validity but miss emergent social behaviors, while field deployments capture ecological validity but confound multiple variables. By combining both, we triangulate our findings across complementary methodological strengths.

\subsection{Prototypes}

We designed and built six functional MR prototypes spanning three form factors---handheld, neck-hung, and stationary---each optimized for temporal wearing (Table~\ref{tab:prototypes}). All prototypes use optical see-through or video see-through displays to deliver spatially registered augmented content, and all were designed to achieve sub-two-second transition costs (onset + offset). Detailed technical specifications, construction processes, and design rationale for each prototype appear in Appendix~A.

\subsection{Phase 1: Controlled Lab Study}

The lab study was designed to establish baseline measures of temporal wearing behavior under controlled conditions and, critically, to compare temporal wearing against a session-wear condition.

\subsubsection{Participants.}
We recruited N=20 participants ([PLACEHOLDER: age range, gender distribution, prior MR experience]) through [PLACEHOLDER: recruitment method]. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The study was approved by [PLACEHOLDER: ethics board] (protocol \#[PLACEHOLDER]).

\subsubsection{Design.}
We employed a within-subjects design with four conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Handheld} ([PROTOTYPE A]): Temporal wearing via handheld viewer.
    \item \textbf{Neck-hung} ([PROTOTYPE C]): Temporal wearing via lanyard-suspended viewer.
    \item \textbf{Stationary} ([PROTOTYPE E]): Temporal wearing via plinth-mounted viewfinder.
    \item \textbf{Session-wear baseline}: The same augmented content presented on a conventional head-mounted display ([PLACEHOLDER: device model]), worn continuously for the duration of the interaction.
\end{enumerate}

The session-wear baseline uses identical content to the three temporal wearing conditions, allowing direct comparison of the same perceptual experience delivered through temporal versus session wearing. Condition order was counterbalanced across participants using a balanced Latin square design.

\subsubsection{Procedure.}
Each participant completed all four conditions in a single session lasting approximately [PLACEHOLDER: 60--90] minutes. For each condition, participants were given the device and instructed to ``explore the augmented content at your own pace.'' No time limit was imposed; participants chose when to stop. Between conditions, participants completed the dependent measures described below and took a brief rest ([PLACEHOLDER: 2--3 minutes]).

\subsubsection{Measures.}
We collected four categories of dependent measures:

\paragraph{Transition Time (Instrumented).}
Each temporal wearing prototype was instrumented to record timestamped don and doff events, operationalized as [PLACEHOLDER: e.g., IMU-based lift detection for handheld, proximity sensor activation for stationary, accelerometer threshold for neck-hung]. We measured onset time (don to first augmented frame), offset time (doff initiation to full return to unmediated perception), total transition cost, and glimpse duration per episode. For the session-wear baseline, we recorded total don time and total wearing duration.

\paragraph{Re-engagement Count.}
For each temporal wearing condition, we recorded the number of glimpse cycles (don--glimpse--doff sequences) each participant performed. For the session-wear baseline, re-engagement was operationalized as the number of times participants adjusted or repositioned the HMD during use.

\paragraph{Social Comfort (7-point Likert).}
After each condition, participants completed a social comfort questionnaire adapted from established social acceptability instruments~\cite{koelle2020social, rico2010gestures}. Items assessed: felt self-consciousness during use, perceived social appropriateness of the device, comfort with the physical act of donning/doffing, and willingness to use the device in a public setting. The full instrument appears in Appendix~C.

\paragraph{NASA-TLX (Adapted).}
After each condition, participants completed an adapted NASA Task Load Index~\cite{hart1988development} assessing mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, effort, and frustration. We adapted the performance subscale to assess perceived quality of the augmented experience rather than task performance, as temporal wearing is experiential rather than task-oriented.

\paragraph{Video Recording.}
All sessions were video-recorded from two angles for subsequent behavioral coding of donning gestures, body posture, facial expressions, and social behaviors (for conditions where a companion was present---see below).

\subsubsection{Social Condition.}
To assess social dynamics, half of the participants (N=10) completed the study with a companion present (a friend or partner who accompanied them). The companion observed but did not use the device, and completed a bystander comfort survey after each condition. This allowed us to assess both user and bystander perspectives on social comfort across temporal wearing and session-wear conditions.

\subsection{Phase 2: In-Situ Deployment}

From the six prototypes, we selected three for field deployment---one per form factor---to investigate how temporal wearing behaviors emerge in a naturalistic setting.

\subsubsection{Research Questions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] How does transition cost affect re-engagement frequency in situ?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How does body coupling affect social comfort in co-located settings?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] What temporal wearing behaviors emerge spontaneously when people encounter these devices in a naturalistic setting?
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Deployment Site.}
We deployed the three prototypes over [PLACEHOLDER: 5--7] days at [VENUE PLACEHOLDER], a [PLACEHOLDER: type of venue, e.g., contemporary art gallery / science museum / university exhibition space] in [PLACEHOLDER: city, country]. The venue was selected for several properties suited to studying temporal wearing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Naturalistic transient encounters}: Visitors arrive voluntarily, move freely through the space, and dwell at individual exhibits for seconds to minutes.
    \item \textbf{Social co-presence}: Visitors typically attend in pairs or small groups, enabling observation of shared glimpsing, narration, and hand-off behaviors.
    \item \textbf{Spatial configuration}: The venue provided a [PLACEHOLDER: e.g., single room / corridor / open gallery] of approximately [PLACEHOLDER] m$^2$, allowing all three prototypes to be positioned within sight of one another and within the field of view of our video cameras.
    \item \textbf{Visitor diversity}: The venue attracts a broad demographic, including families, couples, student groups, and solo visitors.
\end{itemize}

Each prototype was placed adjacent to a [PLACEHOLDER: artwork / exhibit / installation], with minimal signage---a small label indicating that visitors could pick up or lean into the device to ``see something hidden.''

\subsubsection{Deployed Prototypes.}
Three prototypes were deployed, one per form factor:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Handheld} ([PROTOTYPE A]): [PLACEHOLDER: 1--2 sentence description]. This prototype represents the lowest body coupling and fastest transition cost, analogous to historical lorgnettes and handheld viewers.
    \item \textbf{Neck-hung} ([PROTOTYPE C]): [PLACEHOLDER: 1--2 sentence description]. The lanyard maintains passive body coupling when not in active use, reducing the approach phase and enabling rapid re-engagement.
    \item \textbf{Stationary} ([PROTOTYPE E]): [PLACEHOLDER: 1--2 sentence description]. The fixed position eliminates the need to carry or manage the device, with the user's body approaching the device rather than vice versa.
\end{itemize}

All three prototypes presented the same augmented content to control for content effects. The prototypes were counterbalanced across three positions in the venue, with positions rotated [PLACEHOLDER: daily / every two days] to mitigate location effects. No session-wear baseline was included in the deployment; the naturalistic setting was designed to capture emergent behaviors rather than controlled comparisons (which were addressed in Phase~1).

\subsubsection{Data Collection.}
We employed four complementary data collection methods:

\paragraph{Interaction Logs.}
Each prototype was instrumented to record timestamped don and doff events. Logged events included: timestamp, event type (don/doff), duration of each glimpse episode, and inter-episode interval. These logs provided quantitative measures of transition timing, glimpse duration, re-engagement frequency, and total interaction time per visitor.

\paragraph{Video Observation.}
Two cameras were positioned to capture the deployment area from complementary angles: one overview camera capturing the full spatial context (visitor trajectories, social groupings, approach behaviors) and one close-up camera focused on the prototype interaction zone (donning gestures, facial expressions, device manipulation). Video was recorded continuously during venue opening hours, yielding approximately [PLACEHOLDER] hours of footage.

\paragraph{Exit Interviews.}
We conducted semi-structured exit interviews with N=[PLACEHOLDER: 15--20] visitors, selected through purposive sampling to ensure variation in age, group composition, and observed interaction patterns. Interviews lasted 10--15 minutes and covered: initial discovery and approach, the experience of donning and doffing, awareness of bystanders during use, decisions to re-engage or move on, and comparisons between prototypes for visitors who tried multiple devices. The full interview protocol appears in Appendix~B.

\paragraph{Social Comfort Survey.}
Immediately following their interaction, a subset of visitors (N=[PLACEHOLDER]) completed the same social comfort survey used in Phase~1, enabling direct comparison of social comfort ratings between controlled and naturalistic conditions.

\subsection{Analysis}

\subsubsection{Phase 1 Analysis.}
For the lab study, we report descriptive statistics and inferential comparisons across the four conditions. Repeated-measures analyses were used to compare transition time, re-engagement count, social comfort, and NASA-TLX scores across conditions. Where assumptions of normality were violated, we employed non-parametric alternatives (Friedman test with Wilcoxon signed-rank post-hoc comparisons). Effect sizes (Cohen's $d$ or $r$) are reported for all pairwise comparisons. The session-wear baseline comparison is the primary contrast of interest: we report how temporal wearing conditions differ from session wear on each dependent measure.

\subsubsection{Phase 2 Analysis.}
For the deployment, we employed mixed-methods analysis:

\paragraph{Descriptive Statistics.}
Descriptive statistics (mean, median, SD, range) were computed for onset time, offset time, glimpse duration, number of glimpse cycles per visitor, and inter-episode interval, broken down by prototype. Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn's post-hoc comparisons were used to compare metrics across form factors.

\paragraph{Video Coding.}
Video data was coded using a scheme adapted from Brignull and Rogers' framework for public display interaction~\cite{brignull2003enticing}, extended to capture temporal wearing-specific behaviors. Two researchers independently coded [PLACEHOLDER: 20\%] of the video data, discussed disagreements, refined category definitions, and achieved a Cohen's $\kappa$ of [PLACEHOLDER] before coding the remaining footage independently. The full coding scheme appears in Appendix~D.

\paragraph{Reflexive Thematic Analysis.}
Interview transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's six-phase approach~\cite{braun2006thematic}. We adopted an inductive-deductive orientation: initial codes were generated from the data without reference to the temporal wearing framework, then organized into candidate themes that were iteratively refined in dialogue with the framework. Two researchers conducted the analysis collaboratively, meeting regularly to discuss coding decisions and theme development.
