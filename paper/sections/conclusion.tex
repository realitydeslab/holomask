\section{Conclusion}

This paper has proposed \textit{temporal wearing} as an undertheorized design dimension for mixed reality---one in which the act of donning and doffing a device constitutes the interaction itself, with encounters lasting seconds rather than sessions. We formalized this dimension through a three-part wearing duration taxonomy that distinguishes temporal wear from permanent and session wear, and articulated a design space defined by body coupling, transition cost, and social legibility. Through six prototypes spanning handheld, neck-hung, and stationary form factors, we instantiated diverse positions within this design space. Through a two-phase empirical study---a controlled lab study with a session-wear baseline and an in-situ deployment at [VENUE PLACEHOLDER]---we found that temporal wearing produces interaction patterns that appear qualitatively distinct from session-based MR use, including double-takes, hand-offs, narration, and orbiting behaviors.

Our contributions are fourfold. First, the \textbf{temporal wearing concept and duration taxonomy} (C1) formalize a centuries-old but previously untheorized practice, identifying wearing duration as a consequential design variable that existing wearable computing frameworks have overlooked. Second, the \textbf{design space} (C2) provides a structured vocabulary for reasoning about temporal wearing devices along measurable axes. Third, our \textbf{two-phase empirical findings} (C3) suggest that temporal wearing differs from session wear on transition cost, re-engagement frequency, and social comfort, and that naturalistic deployment reveals emergent social behaviors---shared glimpsing, narration, and orbiting---that single-device session wear does not readily afford. Fourth, our \textbf{six design principles} (C4)---Design for Discovery, Instant-On, Graceful Doff, Social Legibility, Invitation to Return, and Shared Glimpsing---offer actionable guidance grounded in empirical findings from both study phases.

More broadly, our findings suggest that wearing duration deserves more design attention than it has received. The history of optical instruments---lorgnettes, stereoscopes, viewfinders, View-Masters---indicates that brief, purposeful encounters with augmented perception have been valued for centuries. The contemporary pursuit of all-day, always-on wearable computing is a valid design direction, but it is not the only one. The full spectrum of wearing durations---from seconds to days---merits systematic study, and the brief end of that spectrum has been largely neglected.

We envision a future in which wearable computing embraces this full spectrum. Alongside devices designed to be worn and forgotten, there should be devices designed to be picked up and put down---devices that invite glimpses, reward returns, and support the social sharing of augmented perception. A visitor raises a viewer to her eyes. For a few seconds, the world is more than it appears. She lowers it, turns to her companion, and says: ``You have to see this.'' That moment---brief, shared, complete---is what temporal wearing is for.
