# Social Acceptability and Temporal Wearing

*How does brief, purposeful wearing reduce social stigma compared to permanent HMD use?*

## 1. The Social Acceptability Problem in Wearable Computing

Wearable computing faces a persistent adoption barrier: social acceptability. Devices worn on the body — especially on the head and face — create social tensions related to:

- **Privacy concerns** (cameras, recording capabilities)
- **Social isolation** (wearer appears disconnected from co-present others)
- **Aesthetic stigma** (devices appear unfashionable or clinical)
- **Power asymmetry** (wearers have capabilities invisible to bystanders)

Temporal wearing potentially addresses all four concerns through brevity and legibility.

## 2. Key Literature

### 2.1 Koelle et al. (2020) [DOI: 10.1145/3313831.3376162] — Social Acceptability in HCI: A Survey
**Venue:** CHI '20 | **Authors:** Marion Koelle, Swamy Ananthanarayan, Susanne Boll

The most comprehensive survey of social acceptability research in HCI (analyzing works 2000–2018). Key findings:

- Social acceptability depends on **context** (public vs. private), **audience** (strangers vs. friends), **device location** (body placement), and **interaction modality** (gesture type)
- Most studies use **online surveys** or **field experiments with researcher present**
- Few studies examine **temporal factors** — how duration of device use affects acceptability

**Gap for temporal wearing:** The survey does not identify wearing duration as a variable. This is an opportunity: temporal wearing may represent a distinct acceptability profile.

### 2.2 Koelle et al. (2017) [DOI: 10.1145/2785830.2785842] — Adoption of Data Glasses
**Venue:** ISWC '17

Examines whether familiarization over time improves attitudes toward data glasses. Findings:
- Initial negative attitudes are prevalent
- Some improvement with familiarization, but concerns about **privacy** and **social appropriateness** persist
- Even extended exposure doesn't fully normalize always-worn smart glasses

**Implication for temporal wearing:** If permanent wearing can't overcome acceptability barriers through familiarization, *temporal wearing offers an alternative*: reduce duration rather than attempting normalization.

### 2.3 Profita et al. (2013) [DOI: 10.1145/2493988.2494331] — On-Body Technology in Public
**Venue:** ISWC '13 | **Authors:** Halley Profita, James Clawson, Scott Gilliland, Clint Zeagler, Thad Starner, Jim Budd, Ellen Yi-Luen Do

Case study of interacting with e-textile wearable technology in public. Key findings:
- **Body location matters:** Wrist-based interactions perceived as more acceptable than shoulder or torso
- **Subtlety matters:** Discrete interactions preferred over conspicuous ones
- **Social context matters:** Same interaction acceptable in some settings but not others

**Implication for temporal wearing:** Temporal wearing can leverage *socially visible but brief* interactions. The brevity signals purpose and temporality, making even conspicuous body locations (like raising a device to the eyes) acceptable because the act is clearly bounded.

### 2.4 Profita et al. (2016) [DOI: 10.1145/2904092.2904101] — The AT Effect
**Venue:** CHI '16

How disability context affects perceived social acceptability of HMD use. When HMDs are perceived as assistive technology, social acceptability increases significantly.

**Implication:** Social acceptability is modulated by *perceived purpose*. Temporal wearing in exhibition/museum contexts benefits from clear purpose: "looking at the exhibit through a device" is immediately legible.

### 2.5 Goodman et al. (2019) — Social Tensions with HMDs for Accessibility
Documents social barriers to HMD adoption even when functionally beneficial. Highlights the tension between utility and social cost.

**Implication:** Temporal wearing reduces social cost by minimizing exposure time. A 5-second peek through a viewer creates far less social tension than wearing an HMD for an hour.

### 2.6 Koelle et al. (2019) [DOI: 10.1145/3290607.3312837] — Wearable Camera Social Acceptability In-the-Wild
**Venue:** CHI EA '19

Field evaluation of a wearable camera's social acceptability. Found that acceptability depends heavily on bystander awareness and perceived recording duration.

**Implication:** Duration of perceived device activity directly affects bystander comfort. Temporal wearing minimizes this window.

### 2.7 Kelly & Gilbert (2018) [DOI: 10.1177/1541931218621237] — The Wearer, the Device, and Its Use
**Venue:** Human Factors and Ergonomics Society

Advances in understanding social acceptability of wearables. Proposes that acceptability is a function of three factors: the wearer's identity, the device's appearance, and the use context.

**Connection to temporal wearing:** Temporal wearing adds a fourth factor: *duration*. Brief, purposeful use may override negative perceptions of device appearance.

## 3. The Temporal Wearing Advantage

| Acceptability Factor | Permanent Wear | Session Wear | Temporal Wear |
|---------------------|----------------|--------------|---------------|
| Privacy concern | High (always recording?) | Medium | Low (seconds only) |
| Social isolation | High (constant disconnection) | Medium | Minimal (brief, shared) |
| Aesthetic burden | High (must look good always) | Medium | Low (seconds of appearance) |
| Bystander discomfort | High (continuous monitoring) | Medium | Low (legible, bounded) |
| Purpose legibility | Low (ambiguous) | Medium (context-dependent) | High (clearly occasioned) |

## 4. Social Acceptability in Exhibition/Museum Contexts

Exhibition and museum contexts are particularly favorable for temporal wearing because:

1. **Shared purpose:** All visitors understand why someone would look through a viewing device
2. **Social norms of looking:** Museums normalize close, focused looking at objects
3. **Brief encounters:** Museum interaction is naturally brief (average dwell time per exhibit: 17-30 seconds)
4. **Turn-taking:** Shared viewing devices create natural social protocols
5. **Spectacle context:** Technology use is expected and welcomed, not stigmatized
