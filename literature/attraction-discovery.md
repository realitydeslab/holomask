# Attraction & Discovery in Public Interactive Systems

*Literature review for "Just a Glimpse" — IMWUT submission*
*Focus: How people discover, approach, and begin interacting with unfamiliar devices in public/shared spaces*

---

## 1. The Honeypot Effect & Audience Engagement

### Brignull & Rogers (2003) — Secretive-to-Social Transition

Brignull and Rogers coined the term **"honey pot effect"** to describe how crowds form around public interactive displays. Their ethnographic study of a large interactive public display identified a progression:

- **Secretive interaction**: People covertly glance at the display, not wanting to appear interested
- **Peripheral awareness**: Bystanders notice others interacting
- **Honey pot formation**: A critical mass of participants draws more people in

> Brignull, H., & Rogers, Y. (2003). Enticing People to Interact with Large Public Displays in Public Spaces. *Proc. INTERACT 2003*, 17–24.

This directly maps to the **attract → approach** phases of the glimpse cycle. The temporal wearing device, placed in a shared space, must overcome the same threshold: people must notice it, see others using it, and feel invited to try.

### Wouters et al. (2016) [DOI: 10.1145/2901790.2901796] — Uncovering the Honeypot Effect

Extended Brignull's work with systematic study across multiple public interactive systems. Identified key factors:

- **Social learning**: Observing others' interactions reduces uncertainty
- **Spatial arrangement**: Physical configuration of the installation shapes crowd dynamics
- **Entry/exit costs**: How easy it is to begin and end interaction affects willingness to engage

> Wouters, N., Downs, J., Harber, M., Cox, T., Semifield, E., Hove, K., & Vetere, F. (2016). Uncovering the Honeypot Effect: How Audiences Engage with Public Interactive Systems. *Proc. DIS 2016*, 5–16. ACM.

### Müller et al. (2010) — Audience Funnel

Proposed the **audience funnel** model for public displays — a six-phase framework:

1. **Passing by** — not noticing the display
2. **Viewing & reacting** — noticing but not engaging
3. **Subtle interaction** — tentative, low-commitment engagement
4. **Direct interaction** — deliberate use
5. **Multiple interactions** — repeat engagement
6. **Follow-up actions** — post-interaction behavior

> Müller, J., Alt, F., Schmidt, A., & Michelis, D. (2010). Requirements and Design Space for Interactive Public Displays. *Proc. ACM Multimedia 2010*, 1285–1294.

The audience funnel is a **linear** model. The glimpse cycle reframes this as **cyclic** — phase 5 (multiple interactions) is not just "more of phase 4" but involves doffing, resting, returning. The temporal wearing paradigm adds physical donning/doffing as distinct transition moments.

### Müller et al. (2009) — Display Blindness & Interaction Blindness

Documented two barriers to engagement with public displays:

- **Display blindness**: People learn to ignore public screens (analogous to banner blindness)
- **Interaction blindness**: People don't realize a display is interactive

> Müller, J., Wilmsmann, D., Exeler, J., Buzeck, M., Schmidt, A., Jay, T., & Krüger, A. (2009). Display Blindness: The Effect of Expectations on Attention towards Digital Signage. *Proc. Pervasive 2009*, 1–8.

**Relevance to temporal wearing**: A wearable device sitting on a table may suffer from "device blindness" — people may not realize it's meant to be picked up and worn. The attract phase must overcome this.

## 2. Threshold & Transition Frameworks

### Dalsgaard & Hansen (2008) [DOI: 10.1145/1453152.1453156] — Performing Perception

Studied how people **perform** their engagement with interactive installations. The act of approaching and beginning to interact is itself a social performance — visible to bystanders. Three stages:

- **Noticing** — peripheral awareness
- **Approaching** — committing spatial proximity
- **Engaging** — beginning interaction

The transition from bystander to participant involves crossing a **social threshold** — being seen as someone who is "doing something."

> Dalsgaard, P., & Hansen, L. K. (2008). Performing Perception—Staging Aesthetics of Interaction. *ACM TOCHI*, 15(3), Article 13.

### Reeves et al. (2005) [DOI: 10.1145/1054972.1055074] — Spectator Experience Taxonomy

Classified interactive systems along two dimensions:

- **Manipulations**: Hidden vs. revealed (what the user does)
- **Effects**: Hidden vs. revealed (what happens)

Four categories: secretive, expressive, magical, suspenseful. This taxonomy maps directly onto how a temporal wearing device appears to bystanders during the glimpse cycle:

- **Donning** = visible manipulation, hidden effects (magical — bystanders see someone put on a device but cannot see what they see)
- **Glimpsing** = hidden manipulation (subtle head movements), hidden effects (magical/secretive)

> Reeves, S., Benford, S., O'Malley, C., & Fraser, M. (2005). Designing the Spectator Experience. *Proc. CHI 2005*, 741–750. ACM.

### Michelis & Müller (2011) — The Audience Funnel

Refined the funnel model with field observations at the Magical Mirror installation. Documented how people transition between phases and noted that **social facilitation** is key — people are more likely to cross thresholds when others are present.

> Michelis, D., & Müller, J. (2011). The Audience Funnel: Observations of Gesture Based Interaction with Multiple Large Displays in a City Center. *International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction*, 27(6), 562–579.

## 3. Museum & Exhibition Studies

### Serrell (1997) [DOI: 10.1111/j.2151-6952.1997.tb01292.x] — Paying Attention

Landmark study of visitor behavior across 108 museum exhibitions. Key metrics:

- **Sweep Rate Index (SRI)**: How quickly visitors move through an exhibition
- **Percentage of Diligent Visitors (%DV)**: Proportion who stop at >50% of exhibits
- **Holding power**: Duration of engagement at individual exhibits
- **Attracting power**: Percentage of visitors who stop at a given exhibit

> Serrell, B. (1997). Paying Attention: The Duration and Allocation of Visitors' Time in Museum Exhibitions. *Curator: The Museum Journal*, 40(2), 108–125.

**Key finding**: Most visitors spend far less time than designers expect. The median visit is about 20 minutes regardless of exhibition size. This supports the temporal wearing concept — brief, intense engagement (glimpses) rather than sustained use.

### Sandifer (2003) [DOI: 10.1002/tea.10068] — Technological vs. Non-Technological Exhibits

Found that interactive technology exhibits have **higher attracting power but lower holding power** than traditional exhibits. Visitors are drawn to them but spend less time.

> Sandifer, C. (2003). Technological Novelty and Open-Endedness: Two Characteristics of Interactive Exhibits That Contribute to the Holding of Visitor Attention. *Journal of Research in Science Teaching*, 40(2), 121–137.

### Bitgood (2010) [DOI: 10.4324/9781315433455] — Attention and Value Model

Proposed a three-stage model of visitor attention:

1. **Capture** — something grabs attention
2. **Focus** — sustained engagement
3. **Engage** — deep processing

The transition between stages is governed by **perceived utility/value** vs. **perceived cost** (effort, social risk, time). This cost-benefit framing applies to temporal wearing: picking up and donning a device has higher cost than looking at a screen.

> Bitgood, S. (2010). An Attention-Value Model of Museum Visitors. *Center for Advancement of Informal Science Education*.

## 4. Affordances for Unfamiliar Devices

### Norman (1988, 2013) — Perceived Affordances

The temporal wearing device must communicate its affordance — that it is meant to be picked up, worn, looked through, and put down. Norman's framework of **signifiers** (not just affordances) is critical: the device must signal its intended use.

> Norman, D. A. (2013). *The Design of Everyday Things* (Revised Edition). Basic Books.

### Hornecker & Buur (2006) [DOI: 10.1145/1124772.1124838] — Tangible Interaction Framework

Framework for designing tangible/embodied interfaces in shared spaces. Four themes:

- **Tangible manipulation**: Physical form invites handling
- **Spatial interaction**: The arrangement of objects in space shapes approach
- **Embodied facilitation**: The physicality of the interface scaffolds action
- **Expressive representation**: Physical form communicates meaning

> Hornecker, E., & Buur, J. (2006). Getting a Grip on Tangible Interaction: A Framework on Physical Space and Social Interaction. *Proc. CHI 2006*, 437–446. ACM.

**Relevance**: The temporal wearing device is fundamentally a tangible artifact. Its physical form, placement, and spatial relationship to users determines the attract and approach phases.

## 5. Social Acceptability of Public Device Use

### Koelle et al. (2020) [DOI: 10.1145/3313831.3376162] — Social Acceptability in HCI

Comprehensive survey of social acceptability research for wearable and mobile devices. Key finding: social acceptability depends heavily on **context**, **spectator familiarity**, and **perceived social norms**.

> Koelle, M., Ananthanarayan, S., & Boll, S. (2020). Social Acceptability in HCI: A Survey of Methods, Measures, and Design Strategies. *Proc. CHI 2020*, Article 318. ACM.

### Rico & Brewster (2010) [DOI: 10.1145/1753326.1753458] — Gesture Acceptability

Found that people's willingness to perform gestures in public is strongly influenced by location (private vs. public), audience (strangers vs. friends), and perceived social norms.

> Rico, J., & Brewster, S. (2010). Usable Gestures for Mobile Interfaces: Evaluating Social Acceptability. *Proc. CHI 2010*, 887–896. ACM.

**For temporal wearing**: The donning and doffing of a head-worn device is a highly visible social act. The brief duration of "glimpsing" may actually reduce social cost — you are only wearing it for moments, not committing to sustained, conspicuous use.

## Summary: Implications for Temporal Wearing

The attraction and discovery literature reveals that the **attract → approach → don** sequence in the glimpse cycle faces several well-documented challenges:

| Challenge | Literature | Temporal Wearing Response |
|-----------|-----------|--------------------------|
| Device blindness | Müller et al. (2009) | Conspicuous placement, social demonstration |
| Social threshold | Brignull & Rogers (2003) | Honey pot effect — others' use invites engagement |
| Interaction cost | Bitgood (2010) [DOI: 10.4324/9781315433455] | Brief glimpses lower commitment cost |
| Spectator discomfort | Koelle et al. (2020) [DOI: 10.1145/3313831.3376162] | Transient wearing reduces conspicuousness |
| Affordance legibility | Norman (2013) [DOI: 10.15358/9783800648108], Hornecker & Buur (2006) [DOI: 10.1145/1124772.1124838] | Physical form must signal "pick up and look" |

The key insight: **temporal wearing's brevity is an asset for attraction**. The low commitment of a "glimpse" lowers the threshold for first engagement compared to sustained HMD use. People can try without committing.
