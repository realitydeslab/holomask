# Historical Precedents for Temporal Optical Wearing: From Lorgnette to VR

A narrative history of how humans have designed instruments for *temporary* visual augmentation, and what this history teaches us about temporal MR wearing.

## Introduction

Long before mixed reality headsets, humans faced the same fundamental design question: how should an optical instrument relate to the body? The answer has never been singular. For over seven centuries, optical designers have created instruments spanning a full spectrum from momentary handheld use to permanent face-mounting — and critically, the majority of optical instruments throughout history have been *temporal* rather than permanent. Permanent wearing (spectacles) is the exception, not the rule.

This document traces the history of temporal optical wearing as a design tradition, arguing that the "glimpse" — a brief, intentional look through an optical device — is the oldest and most natural mode of augmented vision.

## I. The Age of the Hand (13th–17th Century)

### The Reading Stone and Magnifying Glass

The earliest optical instruments were inherently temporal. The reading stone — a polished glass or crystal hemisphere placed on text — dates to at least the 10th century (described by Ibn al-Haytham in his *Book of Optics*, c. 1011–1021 CE). The user placed the stone on the page, read a passage, then lifted it. The augmentation was local, temporary, and task-specific.

When Roger Bacon described the magnifying properties of convex lenses in his *Opus Majus* (1268), he described a handheld instrument: you pick it up when you need it, you put it down when you're done. There was no concept of attaching it to the body. The hand was the universal mounting mechanism.

The Nimrud lens (discovered in Nimrud, Iraq, dated to approximately 750 BCE) suggests that lens-based magnification may be far older than written records indicate. Whether used as a magnifier, fire-starter, or decorative object, its use would have been temporal — held momentarily over an object of interest.

### The Invention of Spectacles (c. 1286)

The invention of spectacles in late 13th century Italy (the earliest documented reference is a 1286 sermon by Fra Giordano da Pisa) was revolutionary precisely because it introduced *permanent* optical wearing. Before spectacles, all optical use was temporal.

But early spectacles were not truly permanent-wear devices. The first designs — "rivet spectacles" — had no temples (ear hooks). They were essentially two magnifying glasses riveted together, balanced precariously on the nose. Users would hold them in place with one hand or let them balance briefly while reading. They were closer to a lorgnette than to modern glasses. They fell off constantly.

The trajectory from rivet spectacles to modern glasses took over 400 years:
- **1286:** Rivet spectacles (nose-balanced, handheld assist)
- **1300s–1400s:** Bridge spectacles (improved nose coupling, still no temples)
- **1600s:** Cord/ribbon attachments (loop over ears)
- **1727:** Edward Scarlett introduces rigid temples that hook over ears
- **1752:** James Ayscough introduces double-hinged side pieces
- **Modern:** Adjustable nose pads, spring hinges, lightweight materials

This centuries-long evolution from temporal to permanent wearing reveals how difficult it is to design a comfortable, socially acceptable permanent-wear optical device. MR headsets are attempting to compress this same trajectory into decades.

### The Telescope and the Glimpse (1608)

Hans Lippershey's patent application for the telescope in 1608 introduced a new temporal wearing pattern: the *directional glimpse*. Unlike the magnifying glass (which augments what's directly in front of you), the telescope augments a distant, specifically targeted view. You raise it, look at something specific, then lower it.

Galileo's use of his telescope in 1609–1610 established the pattern that persists today: look through the instrument for a specific observation, then remove it to record, discuss, or simply rest. His observation notebooks show a rhythm of looking and writing, looking and writing — temporal engagement with augmented vision interspersed with unaided activity.

The spyglass (handheld collapsible telescope) became the archetypal temporal instrument of the Age of Sail. The captain raising his spyglass to scan the horizon is one of the most recognizable gestures of temporal optical wearing in cultural history. The collapsible design — extending the tube to activate, collapsing to deactivate — is an early form of mechanical state transition between "stored" and "active" configurations.

## II. The Social Optics of the 18th–19th Century

### The Lorgnette: Temporal Wearing as Social Performance (1770s)

The lorgnette, attributed to George Adams (c. 1770s), represents a fascinating moment in optical history: the deliberate design of a temporal instrument for social rather than practical purposes.

By the late 18th century, spectacles existed and worked well enough. People who needed vision correction could wear them. But many — particularly fashionable women — *chose not to*. Spectacles carried social stigma: they signified age, weakness, or scholarly eccentricity. The lorgnette solved this by being explicitly temporal: you raised it to look, then lowered it. You were *using an instrument*, not *wearing a prosthetic*.

The lorgnette became a social prop:
- At the opera, raising one's lorgnette was a gesture of attention — or surveillance
- The "lorgnette stare" (pointedly looking at someone through one's lorgnette) was a form of social commentary
- Jeweled, enameled lorgnettes were fashion statements — some had more in common with jewelry than optical instruments
- The *face-à-main* (French name) literally means "face in hand" — emphasizing the handheld, temporary nature

**For our paper, the lorgnette is the most important historical precedent.** It demonstrates that temporal wearing can be a *deliberate design choice* — not a limitation, but an affordance. The user chooses when to augment their vision and makes that choice visible to others. The temporality is the feature, not the bug.

### The Pince-nez: Between Temporal and Permanent (1840s–1900s)

The pince-nez occupied a fascinating middle ground. Clipped to the nose by spring tension, it was more committed than a lorgnette but less permanent than spectacles. Its popularity (1880–1900) coincided with a cultural moment when vision correction was needed but permanent eyeglasses were still somewhat stigmatized in polite society.

The pince-nez's key innovation was the *chain or ribbon* connecting it to the wearer's clothing. This created what we might call a **tethered temporal wearing system:**

1. **Active state:** Pince-nez clipped to nose, providing vision correction
2. **Ready state:** Pince-nez hanging from chain at chest level, not in use but immediately available
3. **Stored state:** Pince-nez in pocket or case

The transition between active and ready states was near-instantaneous — release the nose clip and the pince-nez falls to chest level, caught by the chain. Re-engagement required lifting and positioning, taking 1–2 seconds.

Theodore Roosevelt, one of the most famous pince-nez wearers, was frequently photographed and depicted with his pince-nez in various states — on his nose, dangling from its cord, or tucked away. The instrument's visibility in different states communicated different things: engaged in reading/inspection vs. engaged in conversation vs. in transit.

### The Opera Glass: Temporal Augmentation as Cultural Ritual (18th–19th Century)

Opera glasses formalized temporal wearing as part of a cultural practice. The audience member was expected to:
1. Hold the glasses in their lap or on the seat rail (resting state)
2. Raise them to eyes when wanting to see detail on stage (active state)
3. Lower them to take in the full stage or interact with companions (resting state)

This raise-look-lower rhythm was *collective* — an entire audience performing the same temporal wearing gesture simultaneously. The social acceptability was total: opera glasses were as much a part of theatergoing as the program or the opera cloak.

The low magnification (3×) of opera glasses was a deliberate design choice: it enabled rapid visual adaptation when switching between aided and unaided vision. Higher magnification would have made the context-switch between augmented and naked-eye vision more disorienting — a problem well-known in MR.

### The Stereoscope: Immersive Glimpses in the Parlor (1838–1900s)

Charles Wheatstone demonstrated stereoscopic vision in 1838, and David Brewster created the first practical stereoscope in 1849. But it was Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr.'s 1861 handheld stereoscope that became the dominant consumer format.

The Holmes stereoscope is a direct ancestor of modern VR headsets in form factor — the user holds a binocular viewer to their face and sees a 3D image. But Holmes deliberately chose a *handheld* design over a head-mounted one. In his 1859 essay in *The Atlantic Monthly*, Holmes described the stereoscope as providing a form of "virtual travel" — you could visit distant places by looking through the viewer at stereoscopic cards.

The temporal wearing pattern was distinctive:
1. Pick up stereoscope and card
2. Hold to face — immersed in 3D scene for 10–60 seconds
3. Lower stereoscope, change card
4. Repeat

This was *serial temporal immersion* — brief immersive experiences punctuated by real-world interaction (selecting next card, discussing with companions). The social context was the Victorian parlor: families and friends gathered to pass stereoscope cards around, each person taking a brief immersive "glimpse" and then sharing their reaction. This social viewing ritual has striking parallels to passing around a VR headset at a party today.

The View-Master (1939) later industrialized this pattern, making the card-changing mechanism built into the viewer (the click lever) and the viewing duration even more temporal.

### The Monocle: Asymmetric Augmentation and Involuntary Doffing (1720s–1910s)

The monocle deserves special attention for two unique properties:

**Asymmetric augmentation:** The monocle augments only one eye, leaving the other to see the world unaugmented. This is an early form of what we might call "mixed reality" in the most literal sense — one eye sees augmented, one sees reality. The brain fuses these inputs.

**Involuntary doffing:** The monocle is held in place by facial muscle tension. Any strong facial expression — surprise, laughter, sneezing — causes it to drop out. This means the monocle *automatically disengages during emotional arousal*. The user doesn't choose to stop augmenting their vision; their body's emotional response overrides the augmentation. This is a remarkable (if unintentional) form of affect-responsive wearing.

## III. The Industrial Age: Flip Mechanisms and Professional Temporality (1900s–1980s)

### The Welding Helmet: The Birth of the Flip Paradigm (Early 1900s)

The welding helmet's flip-up visor, developed in the early 20th century alongside arc welding, introduced the most important transition mechanism for temporal wearing: the **flip**.

Before auto-darkening filters, welders faced a practical problem: they needed dark eye protection during welding but clear vision to position their work. The solution was a visor on a pivot that could be flipped down for welding and up for positioning. Some helmets used a head-nod — a sharp downward motion of the head caused the visor to drop via inertia.

The welding helmet established the **three-state model** that is foundational for temporal wearing:
1. **Off the head:** Helmet completely removed
2. **On the head, visor up:** Wearing the helmet but seeing normally (ready state)
3. **On the head, visor down:** Augmented/protected vision (active state)

The genius was separating *mounting* (putting the helmet on) from *engagement* (flipping the visor). Mounting is slow (5–10 seconds) but done once per session. Engagement is fast (<0.5 seconds) and done many times per session. This separation is the core design principle for temporal MR.

The introduction of auto-darkening filters in 1981 (using LCD technology and photodiode sensors) eliminated even the flip gesture. The filter responds to arc light in approximately 1/25,000th of a second — the welder's vision is automatically protected the instant they strike an arc. This represents the ultimate evolution of temporal transition: fully automated, zero-latency, environment-responsive.

### Night Vision Goggles: Military-Grade Temporal Wearing (1940s–Present)

NVGs represent the most extensively researched temporal wearing system. The U.S. military has invested decades of human factors research into optimizing the transition between aided and unaided night vision.

Key developments:
- **Generation 0 (WWII):** Active infrared systems, bulky, weapon-mounted — essentially handheld/stationary
- **Generation I (1960s, Vietnam):** Passive starlight scopes, still mostly weapon-mounted
- **Generation II (1970s):** First helmet-mounted goggles (AN/PVS-5)
- **Generation III (1980s–present):** AN/PVS-7, AN/PVS-14, GPNVG-18 — sophisticated flip-mount systems

The AN/PVS-14 monocular is particularly interesting: it provides night vision to *one* eye while leaving the other unaided, similar to the monocle's asymmetric augmentation. This preserves some natural night vision in the unaided eye and allows for easier transition when flipping the device up.

Military research findings relevant to temporal MR:
- **Transition adaptation time:** After flipping NVGs up, dark adaptation takes 15–30 seconds for functional vision, up to 30 minutes for full scotopic adaptation. The transition is asymmetric and not instantaneous.
- **Depth perception degradation:** NVGs reduce depth perception, and the transition between NVG and naked-eye vision can cause momentary disorientation.
- **Training effects:** Experienced operators transition significantly faster and with less disorientation than novices, suggesting that temporal wearing is a *skill* that can be trained.
- **The "NVG nod":** Operators develop a characteristic preparatory gesture — a slight head tilt — before flipping NVGs down. This anticipatory behavior is analogous to the breath before diving underwater.

### Surgical and Dental Loupes: Professional Session Wearing (1870s–Present)

Medical loupes represent a professional wearing pattern: session-based use during procedures, with flip-up capability for quick disengagement.

Modern surgical loupes come in two main types:
1. **Through-the-lens (TTL):** Magnifying elements built into the glasses lens. The surgeon can look through the loupes (center of vision, magnified) or over them (peripheral/upper vision, unmagnified). This is *spatial* rather than temporal switching — different parts of the visual field offer different augmentation levels simultaneously.

2. **Flip-up loupes:** Magnifying elements on a hinged mount that can be flipped up out of the visual field. This is *temporal* switching — the entire visual field alternates between magnified and normal.

Research on surgical loupe ergonomics (Chang, 2013; Konstantinidis et al., 2014) has documented:
- Neck and shoulder strain from sustained loupe use (2–8 hours)
- The importance of proper declination angle (the angle between the horizontal and the line of sight through the loupes)
- Benefits of lighter-weight designs for longer procedures
- The cognitive cost of switching between magnified and unmagnified vision during procedures

These ergonomic findings directly inform MR headset design for temporal wearing.

## IV. The Stationary Tradition: Going to the Instrument (15th Century–Present)

### Peep Shows, Coin Viewers, and Location-Bound Augmentation

A parallel tradition of temporal optical wearing exists in stationary instruments where the *user goes to the instrument* rather than wearing it:

**Peep shows / Raree shows (15th–19th century):** Small boxes with a peephole through which viewers could see painted or illuminated scenes, sometimes with perspective tricks or moving parts. Street entertainers charged a penny per look. The viewing was intensely temporal — a few seconds per peep — and public, with queuing behavior and social anticipation. The small aperture created a private visual experience in a public setting.

**Coin-operated tower viewers (1920s–present):** The Tower Optical Company's iconic mounted binoculars at scenic viewpoints impose temporality through mechanism: insert a coin, view for 1–3 minutes, the view goes dark. This is *externally enforced* temporal wearing — the instrument decides when the session ends, not the user. This design pattern (time-limited augmentation) has interesting parallels to timed demo experiences in VR arcades.

**Microscopes and the postural commitment (1620s–present):** The microscope requires the user to lean forward, close one eye, and press the other to the eyepiece. This postural commitment is visible and socially legible — everyone can see when someone is "using the microscope." The physical strain of the forward lean naturally limits session duration. The transition from microscope to naked eye involves a full postural shift that takes 1–2 seconds and creates a clear perceptual break.

## V. Synthesis: What History Teaches MR Design

### The Permanence Gradient

Optical history reveals a spectrum of wearing commitment:

```
Momentary          Temporal          Session          Permanent
(seconds)          (minutes)         (hours)          (all day)
    |                  |                |                |
 Peep show        Lorgnette       Pince-nez        Spectacles
 Magnifier       Opera glasses    Binoculars      Contact lenses
 Spyglass        View-Master    Surgeon's loupes
 Loupe             NVG (flip)    NVG (sustained)
                Welding helmet
```

Most MR headset development has targeted the permanent end of this spectrum (AR glasses that replace regular glasses). The temporal wearing paradigm deliberately targets the left side — the lorgnette zone.

### Six Historical Design Lessons for Temporal MR

1. **The Lorgnette Lesson:** Temporal wearing can be a *feature*, not a limitation. The lorgnette was preferred over spectacles precisely because it was temporary. Temporality signals intentionality, control, and social awareness.

2. **The Opera Glass Lesson:** Low-disruption augmentation enables rapid context-switching. Opera glasses used low magnification (3×) specifically so users could quickly alternate between aided and unaided vision without disorientation.

3. **The Pince-nez Lesson:** Tethered ready-state design dramatically reduces effective transition time. Keep the device on the body (neck strap, lanyard, magnetic dock) in a non-active position.

4. **The Welding Helmet Lesson:** Separate mounting from engagement. The fastest transitions occur when the device is already on the body — the flip mechanism exploits this separation for sub-second engagement.

5. **The NVG Lesson:** Temporal wearing is a trainable skill. Transition performance improves with practice. Design for progressive expertise.

6. **The Coin Viewer Lesson:** Externally enforced temporality can be a valid design pattern. Sometimes the system should decide when the augmented experience ends, not the user.

### The Temporal Wearing Tradition is the Dominant Tradition

It is worth emphasizing: permanent optical wearing (spectacles) is the *exception* in 700+ years of optical instrument history. The vast majority of optical instruments — magnifying glasses, telescopes, microscopes, opera glasses, lorgnettes, stereoscopes, binoculars, welding helmets, NVGs, loupes — are designed for temporal use. Permanent wearing is a special case, optimized for the specific condition of chronic vision impairment.

MR headsets need not aspire to permanence. The temporal wearing tradition offers centuries of design wisdom about how to create effective, comfortable, socially acceptable optical instruments that are used briefly and intentionally. The "glimpse" is not a compromise — it is the oldest and most natural mode of augmented seeing.
