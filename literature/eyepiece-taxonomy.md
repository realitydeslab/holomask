# Taxonomy of Traditional Eyepiece Wearing Methods

A comprehensive catalog of optical instruments organized by body coupling type, documenting wearing duration, don/doff mechanisms, and relevance to temporal wearing design.

## Detailed Catalog

### 1. Handheld Optical Instruments

#### Magnifying Glass
- **Era:** 13th century (Roger Bacon described magnifying properties, c. 1268); simple lenses known since antiquity (Nimrud lens, 750 BCE)
- **Body coupling:** Single hand grips handle; lens held over object or to eye
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds to minutes — quintessentially temporal
- **Don/doff:** Raise to viewing position / set down. No body attachment.
- **Transition time:** <1 second
- **Social context:** Universal — used privately (reading, crafts) and publicly (inspection). No social stigma.
- **Key design feature:** The simplest temporal optical instrument. Zero commitment to wear. The hand acts as the only coupling mechanism, making it maximally flexible but requiring continuous muscular effort.

#### Lorgnette
- **Era:** Invented c. 1770s, attributed to George Adams (father or son). Popularized throughout 19th century as fashionable accessory.
- **Body coupling:** One hand holds decorative handle; spectacle lenses fold out or are fixed
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds to minutes — temporal by design
- **Don/doff:** Raise handle to face / lower. Some fold for pocket storage.
- **Transition time:** <1 second (raise); 2–3 seconds if folding/unfolding
- **Social context:** Strongly public and performative. Used at opera, masquerade balls, social events. Deliberately chosen over spectacles to avoid "permanent wear" stigma. A fashion statement — jeweled, enameled handles.
- **Key design feature:** *The lorgnette is perhaps the purest historical example of temporal wearing as a deliberate design choice.* Users could see perfectly well without them but chose to look *through* them momentarily. The handle created social distance — you were "using an instrument" rather than "wearing glasses." This is directly analogous to raising an MR headset for a glimpse.

#### Opera Glasses
- **Era:** 18th century, derived from Galilean telescope design. Popularized in 19th century theater culture.
- **Body coupling:** Both hands hold compact binocular to eyes
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds to minutes — intermittent during performance
- **Don/doff:** Raise to eyes / lower to lap. Some models had lorgnette-style handle.
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds
- **Social context:** Exclusively public theatrical contexts. Socially sanctioned — expected behavior at opera/theater. Shared between companions.
- **Key design feature:** Designed for *intermittent glancing* — you raise them to see a detail, then lower them to take in the full stage. The temporal rhythm of use (raise-look-lower) mirrors the "glimpse" paradigm. Magnification is deliberately low (3×) to enable rapid context-switching between aided and unaided vision.

#### Monocle
- **Era:** First described 1720s; widespread 1880s–1910s. Decline after WWI.
- **Body coupling:** Held by hand OR clipped into eye socket by muscle tension. Some on ribbon/chain for recovery.
- **Wearing duration:** Minutes to hours — can be session or semi-permanent
- **Don/doff:** Press lens into eye orbit, hold with brow/cheek muscles. Falls out with expression changes (surprise, laughter).
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds to insert; instant to drop (gravity)
- **Social context:** Strongly gendered (male) and class-coded (aristocratic, military). Public display of status. The act of inserting/dropping the monocle was itself a social gesture.
- **Key design feature:** The monocle's *instability* is its most interesting temporal property. It naturally falls out — requiring repeated re-donning. This creates an involuntary temporal wearing pattern. The asymmetry (one augmented eye, one naked) is also unique.

#### Handheld Telescope / Spyglass
- **Era:** 1608 (Hans Lippershey patent); popularized for maritime use 17th century onward.
- **Body coupling:** One or both hands hold tube to one eye; other eye typically closed or unfocused
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds to minutes — temporal
- **Don/doff:** Extend collapsible tube, raise to eye / collapse and pocket
- **Transition time:** 2–3 seconds (extend + position)
- **Social context:** Maritime, military, exploration. Later, amateur astronomy. The dramatic gesture of "raising the spyglass" is culturally iconic.
- **Key design feature:** Collapsible design is an early example of form factor transitioning between stored and active states. The monocular nature forces asymmetric vision — one eye augmented, creating a split-reality experience.

#### Kaleidoscope
- **Era:** 1816, invented by Sir David Brewster. Patented 1817.
- **Body coupling:** Handheld, one eye to eyepiece
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds to minutes — temporal, entertainment
- **Don/doff:** Raise to eye, rotate tube / lower
- **Transition time:** <1 second
- **Social context:** Private entertainment, parlor amusement. Sold 200,000 units in London and Paris within months of invention.
- **Key design feature:** Pure visual augmentation for aesthetic pleasure — no practical magnification. The user enters an entirely synthetic visual world through one eye. An early "mixed reality" where physical rotation creates virtual imagery.

#### Holmes Stereoscope (Handheld)
- **Era:** 1861, designed by Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. The most popular stereoscope format.
- **Body coupling:** Both hands hold handle and card holder; face pressed against eyepiece hood
- **Wearing duration:** Minutes per card, potentially hours per session
- **Don/doff:** Hold to face, slide cards in/out / set down on table
- **Transition time:** 2–3 seconds to engage; instant to lower
- **Social context:** Private/domestic — Victorian parlor entertainment. Shared socially (passing cards). The "television of the 19th century."
- **Key design feature:** Created immersive 3D visual experience from a handheld device. The face-press engagement is a precursor to VR headset coupling. Holmes deliberately made it handheld rather than table-mounted for accessibility, and intentionally did not patent it for democratic access.

#### View-Master
- **Era:** 1939, invented by William Gruber. Manufactured by Sawyer's, later Mattel.
- **Body coupling:** Both hands hold to eyes; binocular viewer pressed against face
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds per image, minutes per reel — temporal
- **Don/doff:** Raise to eyes, click lever to advance / lower
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds
- **Social context:** Consumer entertainment, tourism, education. Personal/solo device. Later primarily children's toy.
- **Key design feature:** Modernized the stereoscope for mass market. The reel-advance mechanism (click lever) created a discrete, rhythmic viewing pattern — look, click, look, click — a temporal cadence of augmented glimpses. Direct ancestor of Google Cardboard's form factor.

### 2. Neck-Mounted / Body-Suspended

#### Binoculars (with neck strap)
- **Era:** First binoculars 1825 (J.P. Lemière); modern prism binoculars 1894 (Carl Zeiss). Neck straps became standard early 20th century.
- **Body coupling:** Neck strap for resting; hands raise to eyes for viewing
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds per look, hours of carrying — temporal use, session carrying
- **Don/doff:** Lift from chest to eyes (1–2s) / release to hang at chest
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds
- **Social context:** Outdoor public use — birdwatching, sports, military, safari. Socially coded as "observer" identity.
- **Key design feature:** The neck strap is a *readiness mechanism* — the instrument is always available but not in use. This "ready state" between stored and active is a key concept for temporal MR. The binocular hangs in a liminal position: worn but not engaged.

#### Jeweler's Loupe (neck cord)
- **Era:** 17th century origins; widespread professional use 19th century onward
- **Body coupling:** Suspended on cord around neck; raised to eye socket for use
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds per inspection — highly temporal
- **Don/doff:** Lift from chest, press into eye socket / release to hang
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds
- **Social context:** Professional — jewelers, watchmakers, gemologists. Signals expertise.
- **Key design feature:** Professional temporal instrument par excellence. The neck cord creates the same "ready state" as binocular straps. The eye-socket coupling (held by brow and cheek) is notable — no hands needed during brief inspection, freeing both hands for work.

#### Pince-nez
- **Era:** Primitive forms from 15th century; modern spring-bridge pince-nez from 1840s. Peak popularity 1880–1900.
- **Body coupling:** Nose bridge pinch (spring tension); no ear hooks. Often on chain/ribbon to clothing or ear.
- **Wearing duration:** Minutes to hours — session-based, but frequently removed
- **Don/doff:** Pinch spring, place on nose bridge / release or allow to fall (caught by chain)
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds to place; instant to remove (release pinch)
- **Social context:** Formal, intellectual, political (Theodore Roosevelt, Anton Chekhov). Seen as more refined than spectacles because they could be easily removed, maintaining the "temporal" quality of eyewear.
- **Key design feature:** The *chain/ribbon recovery system* is a brilliant temporal wearing innovation. The pince-nez was designed to be put on and taken off frequently — the chain ensured it was never lost during transitions. This is analogous to a tethered MR headset that can be quickly donned and doffed.

#### Reading Glasses on Chain/Lanyard
- **Era:** 19th century practice; popularized through 20th century
- **Body coupling:** Neck chain/lanyard for rest; conventional glasses placement for use
- **Wearing duration:** Minutes per reading session — temporal/session
- **Don/doff:** Lift from chest, unfold, place on face / remove, let hang
- **Transition time:** 2–3 seconds
- **Social context:** Public, associated with aging and reading. Normalized temporal wearing of corrective lenses.
- **Key design feature:** Explicitly designed for temporal wearing — the chain acknowledges that these are not permanent-wear devices. Creates a visible "ready state" signal that the wearer is a reader.

### 3. Head-Mounted / Face-Worn

#### Spectacles / Eyeglasses
- **Era:** Invented c. 1286 in Italy (possibly Salvino d'Armate or unknown Florentine). Ear-hook temples from 1727 (Edward Scarlett).
- **Body coupling:** Ear hooks + nose pads — stable, hands-free
- **Wearing duration:** Hours to all waking time — permanent
- **Don/doff:** Place on face, hook over ears / lift off
- **Transition time:** 3–5 seconds
- **Social context:** Universal. Initially stigmatized (sign of aging/weakness), now fashion accessory. The shift from stigma to acceptance took centuries.
- **Key design feature:** The benchmark for "permanent wearing." The ear-hook + nose-pad coupling was the breakthrough that enabled all-day use. The transition from handheld (lorgnette) → nose-clipped (pince-nez) → ear-mounted (spectacles) represents the *permanence gradient* of optical wearing.

#### Welding Helmet (Flip-Up Visor)
- **Era:** Early 1900s with development of arc welding. Auto-darkening filters from 1981.
- **Body coupling:** Head-mounted with adjustable headband; visor on pivot
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds per weld — quintessentially temporal
- **Don/doff:** Flip visor down (nod of head) / flip up. Helmet stays on head.
- **Transition time:** <0.5 seconds — the fastest transition mechanism in this taxonomy
- **Social context:** Professional/industrial. The flip-down gesture is instinctive and dramatic.
- **Key design feature:** *The welding helmet is the most important historical precedent for temporal MR wearing.* It separates the "mounting" (headband on head) from the "engagement" (visor flipped down). The welder can transition between augmented vision (dark filter) and naked vision in under half a second with a single head movement. This two-state design — mounted but disengaged vs. mounted and engaged — is exactly the temporal wearing paradigm. Auto-darkening filters (1981+) further automated this transition.

#### Surgeon's Loupes
- **Era:** Magnifying loupes used in surgery since 1870s; modern through-the-lens designs from 1970s onward.
- **Body coupling:** Headband-mounted or integrated into glasses frames; may include headlamp
- **Wearing duration:** Hours — entire surgical procedures (session)
- **Don/doff:** Strap on headband or put on loupe-glasses / remove
- **Transition time:** 5–10 seconds (headband); 3–5 seconds (glasses-mounted)
- **Social context:** Exclusively professional clinical. Signals surgical expertise.
- **Key design feature:** Through-the-lens (TTL) loupes are set in the glasses lens, allowing the surgeon to look *through* the loupe (magnified, narrow FOV) or *over/around* it (unmagnified, wide FOV). This dual-mode vision without physical transition is a spatial rather than temporal solution to augmentation switching.

#### Jeweler's Eye Loupe (Eye-Socket Held)
- **Era:** 18th century onward
- **Body coupling:** Pressed into eye socket, held by brow ridge and cheekbone tension
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds per inspection — highly temporal
- **Don/doff:** Press into orbit / relax face muscles (drops out)
- **Transition time:** <1 second each way
- **Social context:** Professional. The squinting face of a jeweler examining a gem is iconic.
- **Key design feature:** The body *is* the mounting mechanism — facial musculature provides the coupling force. This is the most minimal "mounting" possible, with correspondingly minimal transition time but also minimal stability. Duration is self-limiting: facial muscle fatigue enforces temporal wearing.

#### Night Vision Goggles (NVGs)
- **Era:** Generation 0: WWII (1940s). Modern Gen III: 1980s–present. AN/PVS-7, AN/PVS-14, GPNVG-18.
- **Body coupling:** Helmet-mounted on articulating arm with flip-up/flip-down mechanism
- **Wearing duration:** Minutes to hours (session), but frequently flipped up
- **Don/doff (flip):** Flip articulating arm down over eyes / flip up to forehead. Remains helmet-mounted.
- **Transition time:** <0.5 seconds (flip mechanism)
- **Social context:** Military, law enforcement. Nocturnal operations only.
- **Key design feature:** Like the welding helmet, NVGs separate mounting from engagement via a flip mechanism. The articulating arm with detent positions (up/down) is precision-engineered for rapid, one-handed transition. Military research has extensively studied NVG transition times, dark adaptation, and the human factors of switching between aided and unaided vision — directly relevant to MR temporal wearing. The "NVG nod" (flipping down before action) is a trained temporal wearing behavior.

#### Dental Loupes
- **Era:** Adopted widely in dentistry from 1980s onward
- **Body coupling:** Glasses-frame mounted with flip-up capability; sometimes headband
- **Wearing duration:** Hours (session) — worn throughout procedures
- **Don/doff:** Put on like glasses; some models have flip-up loupes
- **Transition time:** 3–5 seconds (full don/doff); <0.5 seconds (flip)
- **Social context:** Professional clinical
- **Key design feature:** Many dental loupes incorporate a flip-up mechanism similar to welding helmets — the magnifying optics can be flipped out of the line of sight without removing the frame. This is temporal augmentation within a session-worn device.

### 4. Stationary / Mounted

#### Microscope
- **Era:** 1620s (Cornelis Drebbel, compound microscope); 1665 (Robert Hooke, *Micrographia*)
- **Body coupling:** Stationary on bench; user leans in to eyepiece
- **Wearing duration:** Minutes to hours — session-based
- **Don/doff:** Lean forward to eyepiece / lean back. No physical attachment.
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds (postural shift)
- **Social context:** Professional/academic, private laboratory
- **Key design feature:** The user goes to the instrument, not vice versa. The "wearing" is a postural commitment — forward lean and one-eye alignment. The body acts as its own coupling mechanism through gravity and posture.

#### Telescope (Tripod/Observatory Mounted)
- **Era:** 1608 (Lippershey); observatory-mounted refractors from 17th century; reflectors from 1668 (Newton)
- **Body coupling:** Stationary; user positions eye at eyepiece
- **Wearing duration:** Minutes per observation — temporal
- **Don/doff:** Step to eyepiece, align eye / step away
- **Transition time:** 2–5 seconds (positioning)
- **Social context:** Private (observatory) or public (star parties). Historically solo; modern amateur culture is social.
- **Key design feature:** The stationary telescope represents the extreme end of the "user goes to instrument" paradigm. The augmented view is location-locked — you can only see through it at one fixed point. This is relevant to understanding how mobility changes the wearing paradigm.

#### Coin-Operated Viewfinder / Tower Viewer
- **Era:** 1920s onward; iconic tower viewers by Tower Optical Company
- **Body coupling:** Stationary, mounted on swivel post; user presses face against binocular eyepiece
- **Wearing duration:** 1–3 minutes (coin-timed) — explicitly temporal, time-bounded
- **Don/doff:** Insert coin, press face against viewer / timer expires, view goes dark
- **Transition time:** 2–3 seconds (position face)
- **Social context:** Public tourist sites. Shared device — queuing behavior.
- **Key design feature:** *The coin-operated viewfinder is uniquely interesting for temporal wearing because it has an externally enforced temporal boundary.* The coin timer creates a hard deadline for the augmented experience. The user doesn't choose when to stop — the mechanism does. This imposed temporality is a design pattern worth noting for MR.

#### Peep Show / Peep Box
- **Era:** 15th century Europe (Raree show); popular through 18th century
- **Body coupling:** Stationary box; user looks through small aperture/lens
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds to minutes — temporal
- **Don/doff:** Press eye to peephole / step back
- **Transition time:** <1 second
- **Social context:** Public entertainment, traveling shows, fairs. Sometimes private/illicit.
- **Key design feature:** The small aperture creates an intimate, private visual experience in a public context — only one person sees the image at a time. This tension between public access and private vision is relevant to MR social dynamics.

#### Slit Lamp (Ophthalmology)
- **Era:** 1911, developed by Allvar Gullstrand (Nobel Prize winner)
- **Body coupling:** Chin rest + forehead bar positions patient; clinician views through binocular microscope
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds to minutes — temporal, clinical examination
- **Don/doff:** Patient places chin on rest, forehead against bar / leans back
- **Transition time:** 3–5 seconds (patient positioning)
- **Social context:** Clinical, private. Two-person instrument (patient + clinician).
- **Key design feature:** Dual-user optical instrument. The patient is "worn by the instrument" rather than wearing it — the chin rest and forehead bar constrain the head position. The clinician actively operates. This inverted wearing relationship is unique.

#### Submarine Periscope
- **Era:** 1854 (Hippolyte Marié-Davy); widespread naval use from WWI
- **Body coupling:** Fixed installation; operator grasps handles and presses eye to eyepiece
- **Wearing duration:** Seconds to minutes per observation — temporal, operational
- **Don/doff:** Step to periscope, press eye / step away
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds
- **Social context:** Military, operational. The commander's periscope as operational instrument.
- **Key design feature:** The periscope provides remote vision — seeing what is above from below. The operator sees a displaced viewpoint, not an augmented view of their current location. This remote viewing is conceptually distinct from most eyepiece instruments.

### 5. Flip/Transitional Mechanisms

This section focuses specifically on instruments that support rapid state transitions between augmented and unaugmented vision.

| Mechanism | Transition Type | Time | Actuation | States |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Welding helmet flip | Visor pivot | <0.5s | Head nod / hand | Protected ↔ Unprotected |
| NVG flip-up | Articulating arm | <0.5s | Hand push | Night vision ↔ Naked eye |
| Jeweler's loupe flip | Hinge on headband | <0.5s | Finger flip | Magnified ↔ Normal |
| Clip-on sunglasses | Spring hinge | <0.5s | Finger flip | Tinted ↔ Clear |
| Phoropter swing | Rotating lens disk | 1–2s | Clinician turns | Lens A ↔ Lens B |
| Dental loupe flip | Frame hinge | <0.5s | Finger flip | Magnified ↔ Normal |
| Auto-darkening welding | Electronic LCD | 0.0004s (1/25,000s) | Light sensor | Dark ↔ Clear |

#### Design Patterns in Flip Mechanisms

1. **Pivot Point Placement:** All flip mechanisms place the pivot above the line of sight (forehead/crown area), so the optics swing *down* into viewing position and *up* out of the way. This leverages gravity for the "doff" direction.

2. **Detent Positions:** Military NVGs and dental loupes use spring-loaded detent positions to ensure the optics lock in both "up" and "down" states, preventing accidental transitions.

3. **One-Handed Operation:** All flip mechanisms are designed for single-hand or no-hand (head nod) actuation, keeping the other hand free for work.

4. **Separation of Mounting and Engagement:** The key design insight is that the instrument is continuously *mounted* (on head/helmet) but only intermittently *engaged* (flipped into viewing position). This creates three states: unmounted, mounted-disengaged, and mounted-engaged.

5. **Auto-Darkening as Ultimate Temporal:** Auto-darkening welding filters represent the endpoint of transition mechanism design: the transition is fully automated, sensor-triggered, and occurs in 0.4 milliseconds. The welder no longer even makes a conscious decision to transition — the environment triggers it.

#### Phoropter (Optometrist's Refractor)
- **Era:** 1920s (DeZeng Corporation, later Bausch & Lomb)
- **Body coupling:** Suspended on arm or stand; patient looks through while clinician swings lenses
- **Wearing duration:** Minutes — clinical session
- **Don/doff:** Clinician positions before patient's eyes / swings away
- **Transition time:** 1–2 seconds per lens change
- **Social context:** Clinical, diagnostic
- **Key design feature:** The phoropter is unique because it enables *rapid serial comparison* between visual states. "Better one, or two?" The patient experiences rapid temporal alternation between different augmentations. This comparative temporal wearing — not just augmented vs. naked, but augmentation A vs. augmentation B — is relevant to MR design.

## Temporal Wearing Classification

Based on this taxonomy, we can classify instruments into three temporal categories:

### Temporal (seconds to minutes)
Instruments designed for brief, intermittent use. The user holds or flips them into position for a specific task, then disengages.
- Magnifying glass, lorgnette, opera glasses, spyglass, kaleidoscope, View-Master
- Jeweler's eye loupe, welding helmet (active), coin-operated viewfinder, peep box
- NVGs (when flipping), dental loupes (when flipped up/down)

### Session (minutes to hours)
Instruments worn for the duration of a task or activity, then fully removed.
- Monocle, pince-nez, binoculars (field session), Holmes stereoscope
- Surgeon's loupes, dental loupes, NVGs (during operation)
- Microscope, telescope (observation session)

### Permanent (hours to all day)
Instruments designed for continuous, all-day wearing.
- Spectacles/eyeglasses, contact lenses

### Key Insight
The historical trajectory of optical instruments shows a **permanence gradient** — from handheld (maximally temporal) through body-mounted (session) to ear-hooked (permanent). MR headsets have followed the same trajectory, aspiring to permanent-wear form factors (smart glasses). The "temporal wearing" paradigm *reverses* this trajectory, deliberately designing for the transient end of the spectrum, drawing on the design wisdom of lorgnettes, opera glasses, and flip-up mechanisms.
